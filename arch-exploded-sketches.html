Five ways to pull it apart

You chose the exploded drawing (C in arch-page-sketches.html). These are five versions of that one idea. Each one is a working page in a screen-shaped box. Scroll inside the box. Panel 0 is C as it stands, kept as the control.

What does not change. The four planes, the parts on each plane, the four wires between them, and the text from architecture.json. Every version keeps the ochre flywheel wire, the blocked 0.50 model, and the Kubernetes plane that is mostly not there.
What changes. How the drawing moves, how it is drawn, and what it is a drawing of. Two versions move with the scroll (A and D). Two versions are still drawings that you read (B and C). Version E puts the sheets back on a desk.
Mouse, trackpad, touch. All five versions move only with the normal scroll. Use the switch to see each version at phone size. The harness cannot send real touch input, so the phone size shows the layout only.

The control

C from the last sheet, unchanged. The sheets come apart down the page, one under the next, each with its text on the left.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: the stack is only "exploded" in the head. Below it, the sheets are simply far apart, so the page reads as four separate drawings more than one machine.

A

Pulled apart, live

One drawing that stays on the screen. The scroll is the hand that takes it apart. The page opens on the stack, closed. The first scroll step opens the stack and the wires appear between the sheets. Each step after that slides one sheet out toward its text, and the others go quiet. On the Kubernetes step, the dashed sheet becomes solid for a moment. On the flywheel step, an ochre dot runs the loop. At the end, the stack closes again.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: it is the same fixed-picture pattern as the Skills page. That is good for the family and bad for surprise. The drawing is small on the laptop, because it must share the screen with the text, so the part labels are the smallest type on the sheet.

B

The assembly drawing

The exploded view as an engineer draws it: ink only, on a white drawing sheet with a frame and zone marks. The parts carry numbered balloons instead of labels, and a parts list names them, the way an assembly drawing does. Each plane gets a dimension line, but the dimension is time: "always on", "every Monday", "every merge", "≈ 30 min / month". The five sections become the drawing's numbered notes. Each sheet plots itself as it scrolls into view.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: a reader must look from a balloon to the parts list to learn what a part is. That is how real drawings work, and it is slower than a label. The parts list also repeats part of the Skills page. The look is the most technical on the site, so it must stay warm through the paper and the type.

C

The section

The page is called Architecture, so this version draws a building. It is a cross-section, the way an architect draws one. Serving is the ground floor, where visitors walk in. Monitoring is the floor above, and CI/CD is the workshop in the basement. The flywheel is a lift that brings each new model up to the shop. Kubernetes is a temporary pavilion in the yard, drawn dashed, put up about 30 minutes a month. Each section of text has a detail, an enlarged circle of the drawing, as on a real drawing set.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: it is a metaphor, so every part must earn its place in the building, and a metaphor that goes too far becomes a joke. On a phone the full section is too small to read. It works only as a small overview there, and the details do the reading.

D

The ring, exploded

The page opens on the Home ring: the same four stops, the same logos, flat. The first scroll step tips it back and splits it into four solid segments that move apart. Each step after that lifts one segment toward its text. The Build segment is cut in two: Build is solid, and Ephemeral K8s is dashed until its own step. At the gate, a dead end leaves the ring. At the end, the segments come back together into the flat ring.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: it has the best hand-off from Home, and the least room for parts: a segment holds a name and its logos, not the parts on a plane. The planes are the Home ring's four stops, not the platform's four planes (Deploy is a stop, Monitoring and Ephemeral K8s share less room).

E

Sheets on the desk

The exploded view without the angle. The four planes are four real sheets of paper, seen from above, in the site's paper-card style. They start squared in one pile. As you scroll they fan out across the desk, each at its own small angle, with a soft shadow, and the wires become ink threads pinned from sheet to sheet. Each sheet holds its parts as a flat plan, and its text sits beside it.

Scroll inside the box.

Costs: without depth, "planes above and below each other" is gone; the sheets are side by side. It is the softest and warmest version, and the least like a technical drawing. The angles are near the "playful" line, so they must stay small.

What I'd pick, and why

Having built them, they fall into two pairs and one outlier. A and D are the motion versions: the drawing stays and the scroll takes it apart. D has the best hand-off from Home, and A holds more detail. B and C are drawings you read. B is an engineer's drawing, and C is an architect's. E is the warm one: paper on a desk, and the least technical.

My pick is B, with A's motion. B is the only version where the drawing looks like the thing it claims to be: a real drawing of a real assembly. It also says the most with the least text. The time dimensions put "always on" next to "≈ 30 min / month" in the drawing itself, and the parts list is an honest inventory with a number for every part. On its own, B is still. A's mechanics fix that: the same ink drawing, fixed on the screen, opening as you scroll and sliding each sheet out to its note. The parts list and the title block close the page. I can build that combination next.

If the metaphor appeals more than the drawing, C is the most original of the five. The lift and the pavilion in the yard are the two images people will remember.
