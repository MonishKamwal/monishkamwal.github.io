Architecture — sketch sheet

Four ways to draw it

The same system, drawn four different ways, on the page's own sage ground. Pick one and I'll build it out — the chapters below the overview will follow whichever grammar wins. A fifth section at the end asks a separate question: whether the tool tiles wear real brand logos.

What every version has to say — four planes: serving (always on, about $0 a month), CI/CD, monitoring, and a Kubernetes cluster that only exists ~30 minutes a month. A visitor's drawing enters at the serving plane. Serving feeds monitoring, monitoring feeds CI/CD, and CI/CD deploys back to serving: the loop closes with no human in it. Kubernetes hangs off CI/CD and is drawn dashed, because most of the time it isn't there.

The ring

The loop is the drawing. One ink circle, the planes sitting on it as breaks in the line, arrows carrying you round. Nothing to read left-to-right — you see the cycle before you read a word.

Costs: the least room for detail — a plane gets a name and three words. It's also the hardest one to keep readable on a phone; the ring has to shrink or unroll.

The column

Top to bottom, one plane per card, with what travels between them written on the connectors. The loop closes as a line running back up the left margin. Calm, and it survives being read on a phone without changing shape.

The strata

Each plane is a band across the page with its parts listed inside it, so the word "plane" is literally what you see. A spine down the left threads them together and carries the loop back up.

Costs: the most room for real detail, and the least charm — it reads like a systems textbook rather than like the rest of this site. Wide bands also mean the phone version is mostly wrapping.

The stanza

No boxes at all. The planes are set as display type, the way the rest of the site sets its titles, with one thin line threading down the margin and a long curve carrying the loop back to the top. The page's own voice, applied to a diagram.

What to look for. The real marks are solid shapes and ours are hairlines, so even in plain ink the logos sit heavier than the two hand-drawn tiles beside them. In colour they stop being a diagram and become a sponsor wall — four hues arriving on a page whose whole palette is ink plus one ochre.

Two things only show up once they're drawn. Some of these are wordmarks, not pictures — DVC and MLflow put the product's name inside the tile, and the tile already has the name written under it, so it reads twice. And the intricate ones lose their detail at this size: Kubernetes, Grafana and GitHub Actions were drawn to be read much larger than 24px.

Using the marks is fine — they're CC0 from Simple Icons, and showing a company's mark to say "this project runs on it" is ordinary practice.

Note on the fifth option: these can be mixed — the overview could be A while the chapters below it use B's connectors, for instance. If none of them is right, say what's wrong with the closest one and I'll redraw rather than start over.
